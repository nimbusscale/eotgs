# Campaign Guide: Echoes of the Godstorm

## What This Campaign Is About

The world has been slowly dying for centuries — not dramatically, but through stagnation. When mortal civilization turned away from the Old Gods and embraced the New, the natural cycles stopped. Each era has been smaller than the last. Nobody notices because nobody alive remembers anything different.

The player characters accidentally broke this stagnation. They freed Ruin. They will restore Renewal. And when they do, the world wakes up.

Everything that was dormant stirs. Everything that was frozen starts to move. This is not a single crisis with a resolution — it is a fundamental change in the nature of the world. The campaign is about living in that change, navigating it, shaping it where possible, and surviving it where not.

## Cosmological History

### The Old Gods and the Natural Order

The Old Gods have always existed — forces of nature that govern the fundamental cycles of the world. Ruin, Renewal, Death, the Hunt, the Forgetting, the Trickster, and others. They are not good or evil. They simply are. For most of history, they existed in tension with each other — sometimes balanced, sometimes in conflict. That tension *was* the natural order.

Mortals lived within this order. It was not kind. Fires, famines, plagues, the turning of seasons — the Old Gods didn't care about mortal suffering any more than a storm cares about a ship. But it was survivable, and it was cyclical. Things ended. Things began again.

### The Emanations

Where an Old God's nature concentrates, it produces emanations — physical manifestations of divine force in the world. The Xan-Kor is an emanation of the God of Forgetting: a construct that erases things from existence by removing them backward through time. The Wild Hunt might produce a wolf of extraordinary power. Some emanations are vast and world-shaking. Others are small in stature but devastating in impact. They are not tools the Old Gods built — they are what happens when divine nature takes form, the way a river produces rapids or a fire produces heat.

An emanation's power, like the god's own influence, scales with worship. More followers means a stronger god, which means a stronger emanation. Fewer followers means both wane — but even a weakened emanation is formidable compared to anything mortal.

### The Godstorm

At some point in the deep past, the natural balance between Old Gods broke down. The cyclical tension became open conflict — not a war anyone declared, but a collision of incompatible forces. Old Gods pressed against other Old Gods. Their emanations, followers, and forces clashed. This was not the usual cost of living under uncaring gods — this was reality-reshaping upheaval that threatened mortal civilization entirely.

### The Rise of the New Gods

The Godstorm was the catalyst for the most significant event in history: mortals ascending to divinity. Lucifer was the first — a mortal who became a god of light, preservation, and order. How exactly this happened is not fully established. But in the chaos of the Godstorm, Lucifer carved out a bastion of civilization — a space where mortals were no longer at the mercy of warring Old Gods. That promise, shelter from the storm, is why the Light spread so quickly and so willingly.

Others followed Lucifer into godhood. Not all of them were benevolent. The New Gods span the full range of mortal nature amplified to divine scale — gods of war, of cruel death, of blood, gods born from orcish and drow cultures and from every mortal ambition. They are people who became gods, carrying mortal traits (compassion, cruelty, ambition, jealousy) at a level the Old Gods never had, because the Old Gods were never people.

### The Suppression of the Old Gods

The New Gods didn't just offer an alternative to the Old — they actively displaced them. Each New God who claimed a domain had reason to deal with the Old God who previously governed it. Lucifer sealed Ruin in whiteglass to protect civilization from uncontrolled endings (though notably left Renewal alone, not seeing it as a threat). An evil new god of death sealed the old god of natural death to monopolize the domain of dying and ensure all worshippers served them instead.

The suppression worked through a combination of forces. The New Gods fought and sealed the emanations directly — decisive battles where Lucifer's champions and the champions of other New Gods confronted the physical manifestations of the Old. Meanwhile, mortal worship shifted en masse toward the New Gods, weakening the Old Gods' natural influence. As worship declined, the Old Gods became more dependent on their emanations — and as those were sealed, the Old Gods were doubly diminished. Some mortals still kept the old ways, but they became marginal.

The emanations, like the Old Gods themselves, cannot be destroyed. They can only be contained, buried, sealed. The Xan-Kor was sealed in the Eurulian Vaults (now the Nodrum) after Lucifer's champion defeated it in a decisive battle — a battle made possible because the God of Forgetting's power had already waned as its worshippers turned to the New Gods.

There was a shared arrogance in this — across the New Gods, good and evil alike. All of them believed they could replace the natural order with something better, something more controlled. Lucifer thought sealing Ruin wouldn't affect anything else. But without Ruin, Renewal had no counterpart and went dormant on its own. Without the old god of natural death, the cycle of dying was replaced by something harsher. Every suppression created an imbalance, and those imbalances have been compounding for centuries.

### The Stagnation

With the Old Gods suppressed and their emanations sealed, the world entered a long, slow decline. Not dramatic collapse — just entropy without renewal. Each era smaller than the last. The Imperium gave way to the Middle Kingdoms, which fragmented into modern city-states. The New Gods themselves became less active — Lucifer disappeared, others withdrew or turned inward. The mortal institutions built under divine protection (churches, kingdoms, alliances) calcified. The world ran on fumes and nobody noticed because nobody alive remembered anything different.

Critically, the stagnation affected the New Gods' side too. The heroes of legend — the champions who could contend with emanations — stopped appearing. The Light still produces paladins and knights, but not the kind of figures who once sealed the Xan-Kor. The mortal world lost its capacity to produce greatness because nothing new could emerge without Renewal.

### What Happens Now

The party broke the Seal of Unmaking. Ruin is free. Renewal will follow. And when the world wakes up, *everything* stirs — not just the natural cycles, but the emanations in their prisons, the New Gods in their rivalries, and the mortals in their ambitions. The ice that held everything in place is thawing.

The emanations are still powerful — diminished from their height, but far beyond anything the current era can easily handle. The New Gods' institutions have been hollowed by centuries of stagnation. The heroes who once sealed these forces away are gone — and in the case of Lucifer's champion, literally erased from history. The Xan-Kor, in particular, is unsealing itself: by erasing the champion who defeated it, it is undoing the very victory that imprisoned it. Reality is slowly catching up to the fact that the battle that put the Xan-Kor in its prison never happened.

The player characters are becoming the heroes this era needs — the first figures in centuries with the potential to contend with threats of this magnitude. Not because they were chosen, but because they were there at every critical moment, and because Renewal is giving the world the capacity to produce greatness again.

## The Three Layers of Conflict

The campaign draws from three sources of tension. These are not factions to be defeated — they are ongoing, overlapping layers of conflict that generate story arcs, ominous forces, hooks, and complications. Any given session might touch one, two, or all three.

### The Old Gods

Now that Ruin is free and Renewal stirs, the Old Gods' domains are reasserting themselves. Their emanations — constructs like the Xan-Kor, entities buried beneath standing stones, forces of nature taking form — are waking up. Some mortals still keep the old ways — hedge witches, druidic traditions, rural communities that never fully converted — and their patrons are regaining influence after centuries in the margins.

**The nature of the threat:** The Old Gods don't hate humanity. They don't notice humanity. An emanation waking beneath the Ashen Vale is like an earthquake — it doesn't care about the town on top of it. The Xan-Kor erases things from existence because that's what it does, not because it wants to. The danger is impersonal, geological, elemental. You can't negotiate with a landslide.

### The New Gods

The world waking up destabilizes the New Gods as much as anyone. Whatever rival lurks beyond the Bonewall tests the Light's wards and fortifications along the range. The Light's institutional foundations crack as the erased champion leaves gaps in their history and theology. The New Gods are not unified — their rivalries with each other are as dangerous as anything the Old Gods might do. And the open question of how mortals became gods in the first place hangs over everything — especially now that Renewal makes new things possible again.

If Lucifer returns, that changes everything. But so does Lucifer *not* returning.

**The nature of the threat:** Unlike the Old Gods, the New Gods have *intentions*. Whatever rival waits beyond the Bonewall wants something. Lucifer wanted something. Their conflicts play out through armies, churches, politics, and believers. This layer is about war, faith, institutions, and power — divine conflict filtered through mortal structures.


### The Mortals

The institutions that were supposed to serve mortal kind — churches, kingdoms, the old alliances — calcified during the stagnation into self-serving structures. People follow the Light not because they chose it, but because it's all they've ever known. Other mortal cultures serve darker New Gods for similar reasons.

When the world wakes up, that inertia breaks. Renewal doesn't just affect gods and emanations — it affects *people*. New ideas emerge. Old grievances resurface. Ambitious leaders see opportunity in the chaos. Established powers cling to what they have. And somewhere in the upheaval is the possibility that mortal kind could reclaim the agency they once had — not as subjects of gods, old or new, but as a force in their own right.

This is a renaissance and a crisis simultaneously. The stagnation was suffocating, but it was stable. What replaces it is alive but unpredictable.

**The nature of the threat:** Mortal conflicts are personal, political, and immediate. Lords scheme for advantage. Military commanders struggle to hold order. Religious authorities enforce orthodoxy while the ground shifts beneath them. The crown must respond to crises it doesn't understand. Refugees flee dangers that didn't exist a month ago. Trade routes become unsafe. Alliances shift.
